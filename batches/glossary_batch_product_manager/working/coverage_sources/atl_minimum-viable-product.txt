What is a minimum viable product? How to build, test, and improve an MVP
By Max Rehkopf, Atlassian Product Marketing Manager
As a self-proclaimed “chaos muppet” I look to agile practices and lean principles to bring order to my everyday. It’s a joy of mine to share these lessons with others through the many articles, talks, and videos I make for Atlassian
Get the free product discovery template
Align your team from idea to delivery. Prioritize ideas, create custom roadmaps, and ensure full visibility across all stages.
Use template
Key Takeaways
A minimum viable product (MVP) is the simplest version of a product that allows teams to validate ideas and gather feedback with minimal effort
MVPs reduce risk, accelerate learning, and inform future development through focused, real-world testing
Building an MVP involves identifying core features, testing with users, and refining based on insights
MVP feedback helps teams decide whether to iterate, expand, reposition, or stop development
Building a full product around untested assumptions can waste time, money, and development effort.
Teams may think customers want a feature, understand a workflow, or are willing to pay for a solution only to discover otherwise during the product launch.
A minimum viable product (MVP) gives teams a faster way to test the core idea with real users before committing to a larger build.
By starting with the smallest usable version, teams can gather feedback, validate assumptions, and make better product roadmap decisions based on what they learn.

What is a minimum viable product (MVP)?
A minimum viable product (MVP) is the simplest usable version of a product with enough functionality to solve a core customer problem and help the team gather real-world feedback.
The goal is to launch quickly, learn how real users respond, and use those insights to guide future product development.
“Minimum” means limited to the smallest useful scope, “viable” means it still needs to deliver value to real users, and “product” means it should be usable enough to test the core experience rather than just a concept.
An MVP is not always a rough or low-quality product. MVPs should be intentionally scoped, stable enough to test, and tied to a learning goal.
MVP vs. prototype vs. proof of concept vs. MMP
While a proof of concept, prototype, MMP, and MVP are used for testing purposes, they each play a different role in product management.

Why MVPs matter in Agile product development
MVPs are an essential aspect of Agile project management and product development:
Validate customer demand: Test whether the target audience is interested in the product and its core value.
Reduce development risk: Validating demand and other untested assumptions helps lower the risk of product development.
Prioritize the roadmap: An MVP helps shape your product roadmap by validating ideas and guiding feature priority based on user feedback. Product prioritization frameworks can help with this.
Accelerate learning: Releasing an MVP helps you quickly learn by collecting user feedback about your product.
Support stakeholder alignment: MVPs help keep stakeholders aligned by providing evidence teams can use to make decisions.
Improve product-market fit: Use real customer feedback to refine the product around the needs and problems that matter most.
How to build a minimum viable product
Building an MVP starts with identifying the assumptions you need to test and defining the smallest usable product that can help you test them. The following steps help teams move from a customer problem to real-world feedback without overbuilding.
Step 1: Identify the customer pain points
The MVP should start with a specific customer pain point, not a feature idea. Teams should gather evidence through customer interviews, support requests, sales feedback, or product analytics.
This market research should follow these guiding questions:
What is the most difficult part of your current routine?
What were you trying to do the last time you felt frustrated?
How do you currently work around [problem]?
Strong product discovery helps teams collect and organize customer feedback before deciding what to build. Product teams can use Jira Product Discovery to connect that feedback to ideas and opportunities.
Step 2: Write the MVP hypothesis
The MVP should test a specific assumption. For example, “We believe [target user] will use [solution] to solve [problem], and we will know we are right when [measurable signal].”
Measurable signals include:
Activation rate
Signups
Trial-to-paid conversion
Retention rate
Step 3: Identify must-have features
The MVP should focus on the product features required to test the hypothesis and deliver the core value.  Distinguish between must-have, nice-to-have, and future features.
What it means
Example
Must-have
Required to test the core value proposition
Users can create an account and complete the main workflow
Nice-to-have
Helpful but not required for validation
Advanced customization or extra settings
Future feature
Better suited for later roadmap phases
Integrations, automation, or expanded reporting
Step 4: Prioritize by risk and learning value
Teams should prioritize assumptions that could make or break the product. For example:
If demand is the biggest risk, test messaging, landing pages, or signups
If usability is the biggest risk, test a core workflow with early users
Chartin impact vs. effort should be a clear factor into your teams’ decisions. Having a visual breakdown makes this even easier for everyone involved.
Step 5: Build the smallest usable version
The MVP should be stable and useful enough for early adopters, even if it’s narrow in scope. Agile teams can break MVP scope into epics, user stories, and tasks to keep delivery focused.
Step 6: Test with early users
Teams should choose users for testing who represent the target audience and can provide meaningful feedback. Feedback should include both qualitative and quantitative signals:
Usage analytics
Customer interviews
Survey responses
Support tickets
Step 7: Use feedback to decide what happens next
The feedback you collect from your MVP should lead to a decision. Don’t just collect feedback, use that feedback to decide whether you should iterate, expand, reposition, pause, or stop.
Possible decisions include:
Continue building: Signals suggest the core idea is working
Iterate: The idea shows promise, but the product needs changes
Reposition or stop: Feedback suggests weak demand or a poor fit

What to include in an MVP
An MVP should include the smallest set of features needed to test the core assumption and deliver value to users. This includes:
A clear target user
One primary customer problem
One core user workflow
A small set of required features
A way to measure success
A feedback collection method
A plan for what the team will do after launch
An MVP shouldn’t include every feature requested by stakeholders, every possible edge case, or a full long-term roadmap.

How to measure MVP success
Measuring MVP success is important because it helps you validate your core idea and determine whether to move forward. Here are some key ways to measure MVP success:
Validate demand: Look for signals that the target audience wants the product and is willing to use it.
Validate usability: Collect customer feedback and review user behavior to assess the usability of your product.
Validate engagement: Look at session duration, retention rate, and other metrics to measure engagement.
Validate willingness to pay: Test pricing interest through landing pages, preorders, waitlists, or other demand signals.
Validate the customer problem: Use customer interviews to collect feedback and validate the customer problem.

Minimum viable product: Frequently asked questions
What should not be included in an MVP?
An MVP should leave out features that aren’t necessary to test the core hypothesis or deliver the primary value. What counts as unnecessary depends on the product, target audience, and learning goal.
How long should it take to build an MVP?
The time needed to build an MVP depends on the product’s complexity, team capacity, and the assumption being tested. Focus on building the smallest usable version that can generate meaningful feedback rather than targeting a fixed timeline.
Can an MVP be too simple?
Yes, an MVP can be too simple. Minimum viable products should include the features that are necessary to test your assumptions, so those features are a requirement. Your MVP should also deliver the core value of the product without being overbuilt.
