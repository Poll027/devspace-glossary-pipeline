How to write a product brief that keeps teams aligned
By Atlassian
Key takeaways
A product brief is a short planning document that captures what a team is considering building, why it matters, who it serves, and what still needs to be figured out.
Product briefs are most useful at the start of product planning or product discovery. 
A good brief doesn't answer every question. It creates a shared understanding for stakeholders to discuss opportunities and decide what happens next.
The best product briefs connect a clear customer problem to measurable business outcomes.
Centralized tools give every stakeholder a place to review, comment, and stay aligned throughout the brief process.
Building the wrong thing is expensive. So is building the right thing for the wrong audience, or without a clear sense of what success looks like. 
A product brief helps teams avoid those situations by creating alignment before anyone writes a line of code or mocks up a single screen. It’s absolutely crucial in the early stages of product planning and works best as a starting point for teams.
This article covers what a product brief is, what it should include, how it differs from other planning documents, and how to write one that actually helps your team move forward.

What is a product brief?
A product brief is a concise planning document that explains what a team is considering building, why it matters, who it serves, what outcomes it should support, and what information still needs to be validated. 
It's created toward the beginning of the product planning or product discovery stage, often before a formal decision has been made to build anything at all. A well-written product brief is useful across product, design, engineering, marketing, sales, support, and leadership. 
The brief doesn't need to contain every technical detail or answer every open question. The goal is to create enough alignment for stakeholders to determine opportunities and what needs to follow.
What is the purpose of a product brief?
A product brief helps teams make better product decisions before they commit resources. It's where assumptions get surfaced, questions get asked, and everyone agrees—or doesn't—on whether an idea is worth pursuing.
Here are the core purposes a product brief serves:
Aligns stakeholders on the problem and opportunity: Before anyone starts designing or building, a brief gives the whole team a shared view of what problem the product is meant to solve and why it matters right now.
Clarifies what the team is building and why: A brief moves an idea from vague concept to something concrete enough to discuss, evaluate, and act on.
Captures assumptions, open questions, and risks: Writing down what you don't know is as important as writing down what you do. A brief makes uncertainty visible and gives the team something to address before work begins.
Creates a shared reference point for prioritization: When multiple ideas are competing for attention, a brief gives stakeholders something consistent to compare. Teams can use Jira Product Discovery to gather ideas, insights, and feedback in one place before deciding what should move forward.
Helps teams decide what comes next: A brief should make it easier to determine whether an idea should move into a product backlog, onto a roadmap, or into a full product requirement document (PRD).

How to write a product brief in 6 steps
Writing a product brief doesn't have to be a long process. The goal is to capture enough information that the right people can have an informed conversation about whether and how to move forward. 
Here's how to do it: 
Step 1: Define the product idea
Start with a plain-language description of what the team is considering, whether that's a new product, a feature, an improvement, or an experiment. This doesn't need to be a polished pitch. It just needs to be clear enough for anyone reading it to understand the general direction.
A shared Confluence page gives teams a central place to capture the idea, add context, and refine the thinking as feedback and new information come in. 
Here are a few prompts to get started:
What are we considering building?
Is this a new product, a feature, an improvement, or an experiment?
What customer or business opportunity does it connect to?
Where did the idea come from?
Step 2: Clarify the problem and audience
Strong product briefs start with the problem, not the solution. Before defining what to build, the team should be able to clearly describe who is experiencing a problem and what that problem is costing them—in time, money, satisfaction, or some other measurable way.
Here are the types of inputs that typically inform this section:
Customer interviews
Support tickets
Sales feedback
Product analytics
Competitive research
Internal stakeholder requests
This is why you must capture opportunities, feedback, and requests in one place so nothing gets lost before a decision is made.
Step 3: Set goals and success metrics
Goals should connect the product idea to outcomes the business actually cares about. Vague goals are hard to evaluate and hard to act on. 
Specific, measurable goals give the team something to aim for and a way to know whether it worked. Here are some examples of outcome-focused goals:
Improve activation rate
Reduce support tickets
Increase feature adoption
Improve retention
Shorten time to complete a task

Step 4: Outline scope, assumptions, and open questions
One of the most useful things a product brief does is make uncertainty visible. Teams often move forward with a lot of unstated assumptions, and writing them down gives stakeholders a chance to challenge them before work is underway. 
A shared document page keeps this context in one place so teams can revisit and update it as decisions are made. Here's a simple structure for this section:
In scope: What the team is committing to explore or build
Out of scope: What is explicitly excluded from this effort
Assumptions: What the team believes to be true but hasn't confirmed
Open questions: What still needs to be answered before moving forward
Step 5: Prioritize the idea against other work
A product brief should help the team decide whether an idea deserves attention compared with everything else competing for time and resources. That decision should be based on clear criteria, not whoever has the loudest voice in the room.
Common criteria include customer impact, business value, effort, risk, confidence, and strategic fit. This is why you need flexible product prioritization frameworks, custom fields, and scoring for teams to compare ideas using consistent criteria.
With these tools, you can build a product roadmap that reflects actual priorities. Teams practicing agile project management can also use these criteria to align sprint priorities with broader product goals. 
Step 6: Share, revise, and connect the brief to delivery work
A product brief should be reviewed by product, design, engineering, and go-to-market stakeholders before work moves forward. This review surfaces gaps, surfaces disagreement early, and builds shared ownership of the direction.
Once there's alignment and commitment, the brief can inform product strategy conversations, roadmap updates, product backlog items, PRDs, and delivery tickets. The brief doesn't disappear once work starts. 
Instead, it becomes the record of what the team agreed to and why.
Product brief example
Here's a short example of what a product brief looks like in practice:
Product idea: A self-serve onboarding checklist for new users
Problem: New users are dropping off before completing product setup. Support tickets show that most early-stage questions are predictable and repetitive, suggesting users aren't finding the guidance they need on their own.
Audience: Admins at small businesses who are setting up the product for the first time without dedicated IT support
Goal: Increase activation rate by 15% within 90 days of launch
Proposed solution: A guided in-product checklist that walks new admins through recommended setup steps with links to relevant documentation
Success metrics: Activation rate, checklist completion rate, support ticket volume in the first 30 days
In scope: Checklist MVP with recommended setup steps and documentation links
Out of scope: Full onboarding redesign, automated email sequences, or in-app chat support
Open questions: Will users engage with in-product prompts or find them intrusive? Is there a version of this that works for different admin personas?

Product brief frequently asked questions
How long should a product brief be?
A product brief should be long enough to create alignment and short enough that people actually read it. In most cases, that means one to two pages. A brief that runs longer than that has probably crossed into PRD territory. 
If you're including detailed requirements, user stories, or UX specifications, those belong in a separate document created after the brief has been reviewed and the team has decided to move forward.
Who writes a product brief?
Product briefs are typically written by a product manager, but the inputs should come from across the team. Design, engineering, sales, support, and customer success teams often have context that shapes the problem statement, audience definition, or risk assessment. 
When should you create a product brief?
A product brief is most useful at the beginning of product discovery or early product planning, before the team has made a significant commitment to building anything. 
If you're still evaluating whether an idea is worth pursuing, a brief is the right tool. Once the team has decided to move forward, the brief informs the roadmap, the backlog, and eventually the PRD.
