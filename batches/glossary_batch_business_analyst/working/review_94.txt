tb1 Suppose a stakeholder dashboard needs to compare branch amounts and show their change over time. Use bars for the category comparison and lines for the time trend. Choose each visual according to the question it serves.
tb2 Use chart types that fit the question: bars support category comparisons and lines support trends over time.
tb3 Place important information where readers are likely to notice it and keep the layout focused. Use color consistently and avoid unnecessary variety or decoration that distracts from the data. Reduce unnecessary views and labels so the intended comparison remains readable.
tb4 Place the most important information where readers are likely to notice it and keep the layout focused. In this hypothetical page, the branch comparison is the primary question, so give it prominence rather than burying it beneath unrelated views. The exact position is a layout choice for the example, not a universal pixel rule.

Use color consistently and avoid unnecessary variation or decoration that distracts from the data. Reduce unnecessary views and labels so the intended comparison stays readable. Removing a distracting label does not mean removing information needed to interpret the chart; the aim is a clear comparison.

The useful output is a visual design whose chart type, prominence, color, and level of detail support the stakeholder question. Styling choices should not replace the answer. A dashboard full of differently colored charts can still fail to communicate which comparison matters if the layout and views do not remain focused.
tb5 Decoration is not automatically information. Unnecessary color variety, views, and labels can distract from the intended comparison. But reducing clutter is not a license to remove needed context; preserve readability while keeping important information noticeable.
