Dispatch staff use the deliverable; sales staff provide instruction input; an operations manager approves the change; a sponsor funds it. Customer-support staff may be affected by changes in clarification work. These are hypothetical roles, not a fixed stakeholder list for every initiative.
tb2 A stakeholder is someone interested in the work or someone whose involvement is needed to complete it.
tb3 Identify people who use, provide input to, approve, fund, or are affected by the deliverable. tb4 Record who uses, provides input to, approves, funds, or is affected by the deliverable. Classify their involvement: some are directly involved or affected, some indirectly affected or consulted, and some reviewers or approvers. Roles can overlap. For example, the operations manager might be directly affected by dispatch outcomes and also an approver. Do not force that person into just one category if both roles apply.

Record each stakeholder’s role and assess their interest and power over the work. In this example, a dispatcher may have strong interest in usable instructions but less decision power than the approving manager. The manager’s formal approval role does not tell you everything about their degree of interest, so keep the two assessments visible.

The output is more useful than a contact list: it explains why each person or group belongs in the analysis and how they relate to the deliverable. Begin with involvement and effect rather than assuming only senior people count as stakeholders. People whose work changes can belong even when they do not approve the budget.
tb5 A stakeholder is not only a sponsor or decision-maker. Interest, needed involvement, and being affected can all matter. Classification categories can overlap, so assigning one role should not erase another role the same person actually holds.
