tb1 Imagine presenting a recommendation about delivery-instruction access. Instead of listing disconnected numbers, build a narrative that makes the findings meaningful to the stakeholder audience.
tb2 A presentation needs a narrative that makes findings meaningful to its audience rather than a bare list of data.
tb3 Structure the presentation around clear headlines, highlights, and supporting detail appropriate to different reader needs. Use audience-appropriate language and connect findings to the recommendation being communicated.
tb4 For this hypothetical presentation, the opening headline states the dispatch problem, the next highlight explains the relevant instruction-access finding, and the recommendation follows with its supporting detail. The exact sequence is an example structure, not a compulsory slide count. Use audience-appropriate language to connect the finding to what you recommend.

A reader who scans the headlines should still understand the main ideas. A participant who needs more should be able to follow the highlights and supporting detail. If a chart or table is included, explain its role in the finding rather than assuming the data alone carries the narrative.

The useful output is a presentation whose evidence and recommendation are intelligibly connected for this audience. The hypothetical recommendation must be supported by the assumed findings; do not add persuasive claims merely because they sound compelling. Keep the explanation focused on narrative, layering, language, and connection to the recommendation, not on visual effects or a mandatory presentation template.
tb5 A bare list of data is not necessarily a meaningful presentation. Also, narrative does not mean inventing a more dramatic conclusion. Connect findings to the recommendation with clear headlines, highlights, and appropriate supporting detail for the audience.
