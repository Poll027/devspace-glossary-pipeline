tb1 Suppose an order-service team is assessing a business situation. Its experienced dispatch staff are an internal advantage, while fragmented instruction records are an internal limitation. A new external delivery partnership may create a favorable possibility, while an external competitor’s improved service may threaten success.
tb2 SWOT separates strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats in a business situation.
tb3 Strengths are internal advantages; weaknesses are internal limitations. Opportunities are favorable external possibilities; threats are external factors that could harm success. Create four labeled quadrants and place each factor according to whether it is internal or external and favorable or unfavorable.
tb4 Create four labeled quadrants: Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. Place experienced staff under Strengths and fragmented records under Weaknesses. Place the partnership possibility under Opportunities and the competitor factor under Threats. These are hypothetical factors selected to make the two distinctions clear.

For each placement, ask two questions: is the factor internal or external, and favorable or unfavorable? Strong staff and the partnership are both favorable, but one is internal and the other external. Fragmentation and the competitor factor are both unfavorable, but they also belong on different sides of that distinction.

The useful result is an organized view of the situation rather than an unclassified list of positive and negative comments. Do not classify solely by whether a factor sounds important. A favorable external possibility belongs under Opportunities even if the team hopes to benefit from it internally. Keep the classification tied to the factor being described.
tb5 Strengths and opportunities are not interchangeable synonyms for positives; internal versus external distinguishes them. The same distinction separates weaknesses from threats. A four-quadrant layout helps only when each factor is placed using both dimensions.
