tb1 Suppose the specific problem is “Orders wait for instruction clarification before dispatch.” A fishbone diagram organizes possible causes for analysis rather than declaring every proposed cause proven.
tb2 A fishbone diagram organizes possible causes of a problem to support root cause analysis.
tb3 Write a specific problem statement, select cause categories appropriate to the problem, and brainstorm potential causes with the team. Place each proposed cause under its category branch to expose patterns; then analyze the proposed causes and agree actions addressing the root cause.
tb4 For this hypothetical team discussion, select categories suited to the problem, such as information, handoffs, and tools. Write the problem statement and brainstorm potential causes. Place “amendments stored separately” under information, “unclear update responsibility” under handoffs, and “difficult instruction lookup” under tools. These category names and causes are example choices, not a mandatory universal set.

Use the branches to expose patterns in the proposed causes. Then analyze them and agree actions addressing the root cause. Do not jump from a busy branch to a causal conclusion without that analysis. The diagram’s organizational role helps the team see possibilities; the later analysis is what examines them.

The useful output is a structured cause discussion connected to a specific problem, followed by analyzed causes and agreed actions. A vague problem such as “everything is slow” gives the branches less focus than a concrete statement about waiting for clarification. Keep potential causes explicitly potential until they have been analyzed rather than labeling the diagram a proof of cause.
tb5 Putting a cause on a branch does not verify it. A fishbone diagram organizes possible causes, which still need analysis before actions address the root cause. The categories should suit the problem, not be treated as a fixed set every business must use.
