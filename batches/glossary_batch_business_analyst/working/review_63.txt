tb1 Suppose two instruction-access options differ in cost and clarity. Cost is measured in money, while clarity uses a rating. Adding 8,000 directly to a clarity score of 4 would mix incompatible units, so define a common rating scale first.
tb2 A decision matrix enables comparison across several criteria using explicit importance weights and scores.
tb3 Define criteria and importance weights, then map each criterion’s performance values to a common rating scale with criterion-specific scoring definitions applied consistently to all options and higher ratings meaning better performance. Calculate each option’s total by multiplying its common-scale rating for each criterion by that criterion’s weight and summing these weighted ratings, rather than raw values in incompatible units, then compare totals for a defensible ranking.
tb4 For this hypothetical matrix, clarity already uses 1 to 5, with higher ratings better. Define cost ratings specifically: 8,000 maps to 5 and 12,000 maps to 3 for the example options, because lower cost is better under the chosen scoring definitions. Apply each criterion’s definition consistently to every option. Assign weights 0.4 to cost and 0.6 to clarity.

Option A has cost rating 5 and clarity 3, totaling 5 × 0.4 + 3 × 0.6 = 3.8. Option B has cost rating 3 and clarity 5, totaling 3 × 0.4 + 5 × 0.6 = 4.2. Compare those totals for the ranking.

The useful output makes criteria, importance, criterion-specific scoring definitions, and weighted totals explicit. The invented mappings are part of this assessment, not a universal monetary conversion. Higher ratings must mean better performance across the criteria, including cost, so the weighted sum has a consistent interpretation.
tb5 Weights do not make incompatible raw units directly comparable. Map performance to a common rating scale with criterion-specific definitions first. Also reverse the scoring direction where needed so higher ratings mean better performance rather than accidentally rewarding higher cost.
