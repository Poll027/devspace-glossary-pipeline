tb1 Suppose the top-level goal is “Prepare an order for dispatch.” Functional decomposition represents that larger goal hierarchically through major tasks and smaller parts.
tb2 Hierarchical decomposition represents a larger goal as major tasks and breaks those tasks into smaller parts.
tb3 Start with the top-level goal, draw its major parts below it, and break each part into relevant subparts. Choose the number of decomposition levels according to the complexity and detail needed.
tb4 Draw the goal at the top. In this hypothetical decomposition, its major parts are “Identify order,” “Obtain instructions,” and “Prepare dispatch information.” Beneath Obtain instructions, place “Find current instructions” and “Clarify missing information.” These are illustrative parts of the goal, not a universal dispatch procedure.

Choose the number of levels according to the complexity and detail needed. If the analysis only needs the main parts, stop at that useful level. If a part requires more detail for the purpose, break it into relevant subparts. Do not keep adding levels simply to make every branch the same depth.

The useful output is a hierarchy showing how a larger goal breaks into constituent tasks. It is not necessarily execution order: placing two parts side by side shows that they belong under the same parent, not that they occur simultaneously or in a particular sequence. Keep the parent-child meaning explicit so the learner does not confuse this decomposition with the process flows taught earlier.
tb5 A hierarchy of parts is not automatically a sequence of steps. Read lower nodes as subparts of the goal or task above them. The number of levels depends on needed complexity and detail, not an unsupported fixed requirement for every diagram.
