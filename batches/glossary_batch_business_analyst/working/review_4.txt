tb1 Imagine being given an old dispatch manual and a contract for an order-service change. The manual says a supervisor approves amended delivery instructions; the contract says the service must retain an instruction record. These are example statements from existing material, not requirements you have independently confirmed.
tb2 Existing documentation is one channel for gathering requirements.
tb3 Extract the requirements, assumptions, constraints, and open questions found in existing material and record them in one shared document with links to supporting context. Confirm the documented requirements with stakeholders before treating them as execution work.
tb4 Review the material systematically and record what each statement contributes. Put the approval requirement and record-retention constraint in the shared requirements document, linking each to the supporting manual or contract passage. If the manual assumes that only one branch processes an order, record that assumption too. If the contract does not identify who may amend instructions, record an open question rather than filling the gap with your own preference.

Then confirm the documented requirements with the relevant stakeholders before treating them as work to execute. For example, ask whether the supervisor approval rule is still intended and which scope it covers. A stakeholder might confirm it, request a change, or leave a question unresolved; preserve the resulting understanding in the shared record. The practical skill is extracting requirements, assumptions, constraints, and questions with their context intact. Merely copying paragraphs into a new file leaves the learner without a clear account of what has been found and what still needs confirmation.
tb5 Existing text is an elicitation channel, not automatic confirmation that every old statement should be implemented now. Keep the supporting context and confirm requirements with stakeholders; do not silently turn an unresolved passage into an agreed requirement.
