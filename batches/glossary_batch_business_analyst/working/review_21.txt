tb1 Suppose stakeholder analysis shows an operations manager with high power and interest and dispatch staff with high interest but lower power. Engagement planning turns those assessments into specific ongoing communication actions rather than leaving them as labels on a grid.
tb2 A communication plan turns stakeholder analysis into specific ongoing engagement actions.
tb3 For each stakeholder group, specify the communication goal, channel, frequency, and owner. Match communication intensity to stakeholder power and interest and review whether the approach is effective.
tb4 For this hypothetical plan, the manager’s communication goal is to resolve scope decisions. The channel is a short decision meeting, the frequency is weekly during requirements review, and the owner is the BA preparing the materials. Dispatch staff’s goal is to understand current requirements and supply feedback; the channel is a shared update and discussion, with a stated frequency and owner. These channels and timings are examples chosen for this situation, not a mandatory schedule.

For every stakeholder group, make the goal, channel, frequency, and owner explicit. Then check whether the planned intensity fits the group’s power and interest. An assessment has practical value only when it informs actual engagement choices.

Review effectiveness too. If a group remains unclear about what is being agreed, consider whether the approach is meeting its communication goal. The plan is not merely proof that messages were sent. It should connect who is involved, why communication is needed, how it will occur, and who owns it. The example stays within communication planning; it does not transfer business decision authority to the person sending the update.
tb5 A list of channels is not yet an engagement plan. Each group needs a communication goal, frequency, and owner as well. Sending an update also does not by itself establish that the approach is effective; review it against the intended engagement goal.
