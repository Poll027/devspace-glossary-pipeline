What is requirements gathering?
Key takeaways
Requirements gathering helps teams define goals, scope, deliverables, constraints, and success criteria before execution begins.
Strong requirements reduce confusion by showing who needs to be involved, what decisions are required, and what work comes next.
Requirements can come from stakeholders, customers, technical teams, operations teams, legal, finance, and subject matter experts.
A shared requirements document gives teams a reliable project record that can stay connected to tasks, timelines, and updates.
Gathering requirements before starting a project gives the initial idea the structure it needs so your team can plan actionable tasks. It helps teams move from early conversations and scattered requests to a shared understanding of what needs to happen, why it matters, and who needs to be involved.
This guide explains what requirements gathering is, why it matters early in a project, which types of requirements to collect, and how to turn validated requirements into a practical project plan.Get Jira free
What is requirements gathering?
Requirements gathering is the process of collecting, clarifying, and documenting what a project needs to achieve its goals. It helps teams understand the expected project deliverables, constraints, success criteria, and stakeholder expectations before work moves into execution.
The process happens early in the project life cycle, often before detailed project planning begins. In software development, the same process may also be called requirements elicitation or requirements discovery, especially when teams are interviewing users, analyzing workflows, or defining technical needs.
Requirements can come from: 
Customers
Internal stakeholders
Leadership 
Subject matter experts
Technical teams
Legal teams
Finance teams
Operations teams 
Strong requirements should be specific enough to guide decisions, but flexible enough to evolve when new information appears.
For example, a team may start with a broad request to “improve the approval process.” Requirements gathering turns that idea into clearer needs, such as reducing approval time, keeping an audit trail, routing requests to the right reviewer, and notifying users when a decision is made.
Why requirements gathering matters during project initiation
During project initiation, requirements gathering helps prevent misalignment before work becomes difficult or costly to change. It gives project managers and team leads a clearer view of what is needed, what is uncertain, and what needs approval. 
More specifically, it: 
Prevents unclear project scope: Requirements help define what the project includes and what it does not include, reducing the risk of expanding work without agreement.
Improves stakeholder alignment: Teams can compare expectations early and resolve conflicting priorities before they affect project delivery.
Supports realistic timelines: Clear requirements make it easier to estimate effort, dependencies, and sequencing.
Clarifies ownership: Teams can identify who provides input, who approves decisions, and who completes each part of the work.
Reduces rework: Validated requirements lower the chances of building the wrong deliverable or revisiting major decisions late in the process.
Creates a stronger project record: Documentation gives teams a shared source of context for decisions, assumptions, and changes.

How to gather requirements in 6 steps
A good requirements gathering process keeps the team focused on the outcome first, then moves into details, validation, and execution. These six steps help turn early ideas into practical next steps.
Step 1: Define the project goal
Start with the reason the project exists before collecting detailed requests. The goal should connect the work to a business outcome, customer need, team problem, or operational improvement.
This is where teams can clarify project objectives, success metrics, expected benefits, and the problem being solved. For example, a team might define the goal as reducing manual status updates, improving customer response time, or standardizing a repeatable workflow across departments.
It also helps to clarify what success looks like. A strong goal may include a measurable target, such as reducing cycle time by 20%, cutting duplicate requests, or improving visibility into approvals.
Step 2: Identify stakeholders and decision-makers
Stakeholders include anyone who provides input, uses the final deliverable, approves the work, funds the work, or is affected by the outcome. Decision-makers are the people who can approve scope, budget, timelines, or tradeoffs.
A good stakeholder mapping exercise should identify:
Primary stakeholders: People directly responsible for the project outcome or directly affected by the final deliverable.
Secondary stakeholders: People indirectly affected or consulted for input.
Reviewers/approvers: People who may not use the deliverable but must validate it before launch, such as legal, finance, compliance, or security.
These roles can overlap, but they don’t always carry the same decision-making authority. A user may explain what the workflow needs to do, while a department lead may decide what gets prioritized for launch.
Step 3: Collect input through the right channels
Requirements can be gathered through stakeholder interviews, workshops, forms, surveys, existing documentation, support tickets, customer feedback, analytics, and recorded walkthroughs. 
The best channel depends on how much context the team needs and how available stakeholders are.
Async collaboration can make this process easier for busy or distributed teams. A video-recording solution like Loom can help stakeholders explain a pain point or process, and study feedback without relying only on live meetings.
Useful requirements gathering questions include:
What problem are we trying to solve?
Who will use or be affected by the final deliverable?
What needs to be included for the first version?
What constraints or approvals should we plan for?
What would make this project successful?
What risks, blockers, or dependencies are already known?
These questions can also support a stronger communication plan by clarifying who needs updates, when decisions are needed, and where project context should live.Get Loom for free

Scattered requirements create confusion. Teams should document requirements in a centralized location that includes the goal, stakeholders, project scope, deliverables, assumptions, open questions, decisions, and links to supporting context.
Confluence is a natural place to organize this information because it gives teams a collaborative workspace for pages, templates, whiteboards, databases, and embedded context. Teams can use pages to document requirements, decisions, and supporting research, while templates help create consistency across projects.

This structure means the document serves as a shared reference point as the project moves into planning and delivery.
Step 5: Prioritize and validate requirements
Not every requirement should carry equal weight. Teams need to separate must-haves from nice-to-haves and confirm what matters most before building the plan.
Common prioritization options include must-have, should-have, could-have, and won’t-have categories. Teams can also compare urgent vs. important, business impact vs. effort, or required for launch vs. future phase.
Validation should happen with stakeholders before requirements become execution work. This gives approvers a chance to confirm priorities, resolve conflicts, and agree on what will be delivered first.
Step 6: Turn requirements into a project plan
Requirements gathering should not simply end with a document. Once requirements are validated, teams should translate them into tasks, owners, timelines, dependencies, and capacity needs.
Jira helps teams turn approved requirements into trackable work. For example, teams can use Jira to create work items, organize backlogs, plan timelines, and connect work to goals.
This is also where teams can use requirements to support capacity planning, resource planning, and a realistic project timeline. Jira Plans can help teams schedule work, allocate capacity, map dependencies, and model different scenarios in one place.
For larger efforts, teams may also summarize the validated requirements in a project charter. This gives leaders and stakeholders a concise record of the purpose, scope, goals, owners, risks, and approval path.

What to include in a requirements document
A requirements document should give teams enough context to make decisions and move into execution. It should be clear, scannable, and easy to update as the project evolves.
Include:
Project summary: A brief explanation of the project, the problem it addresses, and the expected outcome.
Goals and success metrics: The measurable results the project should support.
Stakeholders and owners: The people involved, their roles, and who makes key decisions.
In-scope requirements: The requirements approved for the current project or phase.
Out-of-scope items: Requests or ideas that are intentionally excluded, deferred, or parked for later.
Assumptions: Conditions the team believes to be true, such as available budget, team capacity, or technology access.
Constraints: Limits related to time, budget, systems, compliance, staffing, or approvals.
Dependencies: Work, teams, systems, or decisions that must happen before progress can continue.
Acceptance criteria: The conditions that show a requirement has been met.
Open questions: Items that still need investigation, review, or stakeholder input.
Decision log: A record of major decisions, who made them, and when they were approved.
This documentation should stay connected to the project as work moves forward. Requirements, tasks, timelines, recordings, and decisions are easier to manage when teams can move between shared context and active work without losing the thread.
Start projects with clearer requirements and stronger alignment
Strong requirements gathering helps teams understand what they are building, why it matters, who needs to be involved, and what it will take to deliver the work successfully. It also gives project managers and team leads a clearer path from early idea to approved plan.
Requirements gathering: Frequently asked questions
Why is requirements gathering essential in project management?
Requirements gathering ensures that all stakeholders have a shared understanding of project goals, features, and constraints. This process helps prevent misunderstandings, reduces the risk of costly changes later, and increases the likelihood of project success.
Which techniques are commonly used for effective requirements gathering?
Common techniques include interviews, surveys, workshops, observation, and reviewing existing documentation. Using a mix of these methods helps capture comprehensive and accurate requirements from all relevant stakeholders.
How can teams handle changing requirements during a project?
Teams should establish a clear process for managing changes, such as regular reviews and a formal change request system. Open communication and documentation help ensure that changes are evaluated for impact and integrated smoothly.
What challenges do teams face during requirements gathering?
Challenges include unclear stakeholder expectations, communication gaps, conflicting priorities, and incomplete information. Addressing these issues early with active listening, thorough documentation, and stakeholder involvement can help overcome them.
