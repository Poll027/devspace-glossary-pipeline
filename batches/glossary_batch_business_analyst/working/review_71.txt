tb1 Suppose the same instruction-access finding must be explained to dispatch managers and to technical colleagues. The audience determines the appropriate detail, terminology, and communication approach.
tb2 The audience determines appropriate detail, terminology, and how information is communicated.
tb3 Use the audience’s language and explain an unfamiliar concept with a familiar analogy when useful. Make the main ideas clear in headlines while preserving supporting detail for readers who need it.
tb4 For managers, a headline might say “Missing instruction matches leave some orders unresolved,” followed by the operational meaning. For technical readers familiar with the query, supporting detail can identify the relevant matching fields and the result behavior. Both explanations should preserve the same finding; changing audience is not permission to change the evidence.

Use the audience’s language. If an unfamiliar relationship needs explanation, a familiar analogy may help: a customer identifier can be compared to a reference that points to a customer record. Use the analogy to clarify the concept, not replace its actual meaning. Keep the main ideas clear in headlines and preserve detail for readers who need it.

The practical skill is selecting how much explanation and which terms serve these readers. A nontechnical audience does not require a different conclusion, and a technical audience does not require every possible implementation detail. The example communications are choices for this hypothetical report, not fixed scripts for everyone with those job titles.
tb5 Audience adaptation changes detail and language, not the supported facts. Unexplained jargon can obscure the main idea, while deleting all supporting detail can leave readers unable to understand it. Preserve clear headlines and the detail appropriate to their needs.
