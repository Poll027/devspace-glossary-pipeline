tb1 Suppose stakeholders are choosing between two instruction-access solutions. Define shared criteria before comparing them: instruction clarity and fit with the agreed operating constraints. For this hypothetical assessment, use weights 0.6 and 0.4 and a common score scale.
tb2 Solution comparison uses defined decision criteria rather than an unstructured preference.
tb3 Score each candidate solution against the same criteria and multiply the scores by the corresponding importance weights. Sum each candidate’s weighted scores, compare the totals, and explain the recommendation in terms of the criteria and scores.
tb4 Solution A scores 4 for clarity and 2 for fit, giving 4 × 0.6 + 2 × 0.4 = 3.2. Solution B scores 3 and 4, giving 3 × 0.6 + 4 × 0.4 = 3.4. Compare the totals and recommend B on this assessment, explaining that its stronger fit outweighs A’s clarity advantage under the selected weights.

The recommendation should point back to the criteria and scores. “We prefer B” omits the analytical basis; “B totals 3.4 under the agreed clarity and fit assessment” makes that basis visible. The two totals are hypothetical and depend on the chosen assessment, not a claim about real products.

The useful BA output is a reasoned solution recommendation, not only a completed matrix. State what was compared, how importance affected the scores, and how the resulting totals support the recommendation. Keep candidate solutions on the same criteria rather than awarding one option points for an advantage the other option was never assessed against.
tb5 A preferred option does not become a supported recommendation merely because it is given a score. Evaluate every candidate against the same criteria and weights, calculate the totals, and explain the recommendation in those terms rather than using an unstructured preference.
