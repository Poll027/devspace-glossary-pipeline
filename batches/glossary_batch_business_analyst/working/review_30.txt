FACT f1 A decision table records how combinations of conditions produce outcomes, including complex logic such as business rules.
FACT f2 To construct a decision table, put the conditions and resulting actions in rows and give each decision rule a column containing a unique condition combination and its associated actions.
FACT f3 For a limited-entry decision table, mark a condition T when satisfied, F when not satisfied, and – when irrelevant to the outcome; mark an action X when it should occur and leave it blank when it should not occur.
FACT f4 Check completeness by listing all condition combinations and confirming each feasible combination has a rule column with its resulting action; a feasible combination without a rule is a gap, while infeasible combinations can be removed.
FACT f5 Check consistency by comparing the outcomes assigned to the same feasible condition combination across the written rules and table; incompatible outcomes for that combination expose a contradiction that needs resolution.
tb1 Imagine a hypothetical policy with two conditions: whether an order is urgent and whether approval is present. The written rule says an urgent approved order may proceed; an urgent unapproved order requests approval; a nonurgent order may proceed regardless of approval.
tb2 A decision table records how combinations of conditions produce outcomes, including complex logic such as business rules.
tb3 To construct a decision table, put the conditions and resulting actions in rows and give each decision rule a column containing a unique condition combination and its associated actions. For a limited-entry decision table, mark a condition T when satisfied, F when not satisfied, and – when irrelevant to the outcome; mark an action X when it should occur and leave it blank when it should not occur. Check completeness by listing all condition combinations and confirming each feasible combination has a rule column with its resulting action; a feasible combination without a rule is a gap, while infeasible combinations can be removed. Check consistency by comparing the outcomes assigned to the same feasible condition combination across the written rules and table; incompatible outcomes for that combination expose a contradiction that needs resolution.
tb4 Construct a limited-entry decision table with condition rows “Urgent?” and “Approved?” and action rows “Proceed” and “Request approval.” Give each rule a column. Rule 1 has T/T and X for Proceed. Rule 2 has T/F and X for Request approval. Rule 3 has F/– and X for Proceed. Leave the other action cells blank. Here – means the approval condition is irrelevant to the nonurgent outcome, so Rule 3 covers F/T and F/F.

Check completeness by listing the feasible combinations T/T, T/F, F/T, and F/F. All four have an outcome in this example. If Rule 2 were missing, T/F would be a gap. Infeasible combinations could be removed in a different policy, but none are declared infeasible here.

Check consistency against the written rules. If another rule assigns Request approval instead of Proceed to T/T, the same feasible combination has incompatible outcomes. Record that contradiction for resolution rather than choosing one arbitrarily. The table’s job is to expose condition-to-action logic, including gaps and contradictions, not conceal uncertainty behind a neat grid.
tb5 A dash is not false: it means the condition is irrelevant to the outcome. Completeness and consistency are separate checks. A table can cover every feasible combination and still contradict the written rules by assigning incompatible outcomes to the same combination.
