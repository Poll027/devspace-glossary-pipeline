tb1 Imagine an operations manager receiving a report about dispatch delays. A page of observations alone leaves the reader to find the main conclusion. Structure the report for its readers and purpose.
tb2 A report should be structured for its readers rather than assuming the findings speak for themselves.
tb3 Put the key message in headlines for readers who only scan, then provide highlights and supporting detail for readers who need more. Write at the level of detail the audience needs and select information that serves the report’s purpose.
tb4 For this hypothetical report, a headline could state “Instruction clarification is the main issue identified in dispatch preparation.” Highlights then summarize the relevant findings, and supporting detail explains the evidence available in the exercise. The headline is an example report conclusion based on assumed findings, not a claim that clarification is always the main delay.

This layering serves different reading needs. A scanning reader can find the key message; a reader needing more can continue into highlights and detail. Select information that serves the report’s purpose and use the audience’s required level of detail rather than including every note simply because it exists.

The practical output is not a shorter report at any cost. It is an organized report whose key message is prominent and whose supporting information remains available. If the purpose is to communicate the dispatch finding, unrelated observations about office equipment distract from that purpose. Keep enough relevant detail for the intended reader without assuming findings will explain themselves.
tb5 Supporting detail does not remove the need for a clear key message. Conversely, a headline alone is not the same as a structured report for readers who need evidence. Use headlines, highlights, and relevant detail rather than choosing between a slogan and an unorganized data dump.
