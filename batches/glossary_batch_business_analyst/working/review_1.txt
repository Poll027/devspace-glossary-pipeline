tb1 Suppose dispatch staff describe delivery instructions as “hard to use.” You need to learn what happens during preparation, not persuade them that a particular replacement is desirable. Set the interview aim as understanding the expected outcome, current operating conditions, and constraints on accessing instructions.
tb2 A structured interview follows an interview script, unlike a semistructured interview’s flexibly ordered guide.
tb3 Define what needs to be learned and prepare questions that address those aims. Ask concise, open-ended questions that let the participant describe their experience without suggesting the answer. Record the elicited needs in a shared requirements document with links to the supporting interview context. Probe vague answers with follow-up questions about concrete expected outcomes, operating conditions and constraints, and observable or measurable criteria showing that the requirement has been met.
tb4 Prepare a script with questions such as “Describe how you find the instructions for an order,” “What information do you need before dispatch?” and “What happens when that information is unclear?” A structured interview follows that script; a flexibly ordered guide would instead be semistructured. Keep each question concise and leave room for the participant’s own account.

When a participant says, “It should be quick,” ask what concrete outcome they expect, under which conditions, and what observable or measurable result would show that the need is met. For this hypothetical interview, they might specify finding the current instructions within 30 seconds during dispatch preparation. Record that elicited need in the shared requirements document and link it to the interview context, including the conditions discussed. The numerical threshold is example stakeholder input, not a universal requirement. The finished record should preserve both the stated need and the context that supports it, rather than only your summary of “make it faster.”
tb5 Open-ended does not mean directionless: the questions still serve defined learning aims. Conversely, asking “Would a new mobile screen fix this?” suggests an answer instead of letting the participant describe their experience. A vague answer calls for concrete follow-up, not an invented requirement.
