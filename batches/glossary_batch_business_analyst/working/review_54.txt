tb1 Imagine an operations team wants to improve dispatch preparation. Before defining the desired result, record the present situation as a baseline. In this hypothetical assessment, dispatch information is kept in separate records, clarification calls occur before preparation, and one branch uses a different handoff from another.
tb2 A current-state assessment describes the present situation as a baseline for identifying improvements.
tb3 Record detailed notes about the current business situation in a structured current-state column. Organize the current-state notes by the business areas being assessed so they can be compared with goals and gaps.
tb4 Put detailed current-state notes in a structured current-state column. Organize them by business area: instruction access, handoffs, and dispatch preparation. That organization makes later comparison possible without mixing every observation into one vague statement such as “the process is inefficient.”

For instruction access, the example note might be “Staff consult a separate sheet for current instructions.” For handoffs, it might describe who supplies amended information. If the scenario provides a measured preparation time, record it with its context. Do not invent a baseline number merely because a later target will need comparison.

The output is a description of the present business situation arranged by the areas being assessed. It is not yet a list of desired changes. Keep an observed condition, a proposed improvement, and a judgment about success separate. This way a reader can see what current situation the subsequent goals and gaps refer to, rather than having to reconstruct the baseline from the recommendation.
tb5 Current-state assessment is not a wish list. A note saying “use one instruction record” describes a desired change, not the current situation. Record the baseline in the current-state column and organize it by the assessed business areas.
