Return on Investment: Formula, Meaning, and How to Calculate It
Learn the different ways to calculate Return on Investment
Written by
Tim Vipond, FMVA
Published
February 27, 2020
Read Time
9
                                                      minutes
Earn Your Certification
Every major financial choice begins with a simple question: Does this investment create value? Understanding
ROI
equips you to compare options, assess potential outcomes, and allocate capital with confidence.
In this guide, you’ll learn
how to determine ROI
by analyzing scenarios that account for
initial cost
, risk appetite, and an investment’s projected performance. Join over 2 million professionals who turn to CFI to build real-world skills in accounting, modeling, analysis, and more.
What is Return on Investment (ROI)?
Return on investment (ROI)
is a
financial ratio
used to calculate the benefit an investor will receive in relation to their
investment cost
. It is most commonly measured as
net income
divided by the original
capital cost
of the investment. The higher the ratio, the greater the benefit earned.
This guide breaks down the
return on investment formula
with clear explanations and real-world examples. You’ll learn
how to calculate return rate on investment
using proven methods across different scenarios. A downloadable ROI calculator is also included to help streamline your analysis.
ROI Formula
There are several versions of the
return on investment formula
. The two most commonly used are:
ROI = Net Income / Cost of Investment
or
ROI = Investment Gain / Investment Base
The first version of the
ROI formula
(net income divided by the cost of an investment) is the most commonly used ratio.
The simplest way to think about the ROI formula is to take some type of “benefit” and divide it by the “cost.”  When someone says something has a good or bad ROI, it’s important to ask them to clarify exactly how they measure it.
What is a Good ROI?
A “good” return on investment depends entirely on your objective, the type of asset, and how long you plan to hold it. There’s no universal benchmark; for example, an 8% return might outperform expectations in fixed income, but underperform in high-growth
business ventures
. That’s why understanding
how to estimate return on investment
is key to making
informed financial decisions
.
Here are the most pertinent factors to consider:
Risk Tolerance
: Higher ROI potential usually involves greater volatility. Establish your tolerance levels before committing.
Holding Period
: A high return over a short timeline typically delivers better value than a similar return over several years.
Expected ROI
: Compare your projected results to relevant industry data or long-term historical averages.
Opportunity Costs
: Ask what other outcomes you could have pursued with the same capital.
Asset Type
: ROI expectations vary across categories. A rental property, a startup, or a blue-chip stock each carries different risk-reward profiles.
Rather than chasing an arbitrary number, define ROI success based on your goals, risk profile, and the tradeoffs involved.
Example of the ROI Formula Calculation
An investor purchases property A, which is valued at $500,000. Two years later, the investor sells the property for $1,000,000.
We use the investment gain formula in this case.
ROI = (1,000,000 – 500,000) / (500,000) = 1 or 100%

The Use of the ROI Formula Calculation
ROI calculations are simple and help investors decide whether to take or skip an investment opportunity. The calculation can also indicate how an investment has performed to date. When an investment shows a positive or negative ROI, it can be an important indication to the investor about the value of their investment.
Using an ROI formula, investors can separate low-performing investments from high-performing investments. This approach allows investors and portfolio managers to attempt to optimize their investments.
There are many benefits to using the return on investment ratio that every analyst should be aware of.
1. Simple and Easy to Calculate
The return on investment metric is frequently used because it’s so easy to calculate. Only two figures are required — the benefit and the cost. Because a “return” can mean different things to different people, the ROI formula is easy to use, as there is no strict definition of “return”.
2. Universally Understood
Return on investment is a universally understood concept, so it’s almost guaranteed that if you use the metric in conversation, then people will know what you’re talking about.
Limitations of the ROI Formula
While the ratio is often very useful, the
return on investment
formula
has some limitations that are important to know. Below are two key points that are worthy of note.
1. The ROI Formula Disregards the Factor of Time
A higher ROI number does not always mean a better investment option. For example, two investments have the same ROI of 50%. However, the first investment is completed in three years, while the second investment needs five years to produce the same yield. The same ROI for both investments blurred the bigger picture, but when the factor of time was added, the investor easily saw the better option.
The investor needs to compare two instruments under the same time period and the same circumstances.
2. The ROI Formula is Susceptible to Manipulation
An ROI calculation can vary between individuals depending on which
return on investment formula
they choose to apply. A marketing manager can use the property calculation explained in the example section without accounting for additional costs, such as maintenance costs, property taxes, sales fees, stamp duties, and legal costs.
An investor needs to consider the true ROI, which accounts for all possible costs incurred when an investment increases in value.
Common Mistakes in Calculating ROI
Even when the ROI formula is applied correctly, the inputs feeding it often aren’t. Two asset types account for most of the errors:
Stock investments:
Leaving out transaction costs and brokerage fees overstates the gain, and ignoring dividends received along the way understates it. Both the initial cost and the final value need to reflect everything that changed hands.
Real estate investments:
A sale price alone ignores rental income collected during the holding period and the ongoing costs of owning the property, including property taxes, insurance, and maintenance. Skipping either side of that ledger produces an ROI that looks better, or worse, than reality.
The fix in both cases is the same: build out every cash inflow and outflow before dividing, not just the purchase and sale price.
Annualized ROI Formula
As mentioned above, one drawback of the traditional return on investment metric is that it doesn’t take into account time periods. For example, a return of 25% over 5 years is expressed the same way as a return of 25% over 5 days. But obviously, a return of 25% in 5 days is much better than 5 years!
To overcome this issue, we can calculate an annualized ROI formula.
ROI Formula:
= [(Ending Value / Beginning Value) ^ (1 / # of Years)] – 1
Where:
# of years = (Ending date – Starting Date) / 365
For example, an investor buys a stock on January 1st, 2017 for $12.50 and sells it on August 24, 2017, for $15.20. What is the regular and annualized return on investment?
Regular = ($15.20 – $12.50) / $12.50 =
21.6%
Annualized = [($15.20 / $12.50) ^ (1 / ((Aug 24 – Jan 1)/365) )] -1 =
35.5%
Leverage and ROI
Leverage increases your return on investment by letting you control a larger asset using less of your capital. Instead of funding the full purchase, you borrow money to complete the
initial investment
. This strategy can significantly increase your gains when the investment performs well. However, it can also amplify losses if the investment underperforms.
In any
ROI analysis
involving leverage, comparing both scenarios, cash-only versus financed, is essential to fully understand how borrowed capital affects your returns.
How Leverage Impacts ROI
Let’s walk through an example leveraged
ROI equation
:
Property Value:
$200,000
Sale Price After One Year:
$220,000
Total Profit:
$20,000
Without Leverage (100% Cash Investment)
You invest $200,000 of your own capital.
ROI = $20,000 ÷ $200,000 =
10%
With Leverage (80% Financing)
You invest only $40,000 and finance the remaining $160,000 with a loan.
ROI = $20,000 ÷ $40,000 =
50%
The asset’s
current value
and
total profits
remain the same in both cases. What changes is the ROI, because you committed less personal capital in the leveraged scenario. This example shows how leverage can multiply your
investment’s net profit
by evaluating results against a smaller personal contribution instead of the full asset price.
Benefits of Leverage
Greater ROI Potential
: A smaller capital outlay can produce a much higher return percentage.
Access to Higher-Value Investments
: Borrowed funds allow you to invest in assets that might otherwise be out of reach.
Improved Capital Efficiency
: Less personal capital allows you to pursue additional opportunities or diversify your portfolio.
Risks of Leverage
Losses Can Multiply Quickly
: If the property falls to $180,000, you still face a $20,000 loss. In a leveraged scenario, that becomes –50% ROI because your return is measured against only $40,000 of invested capital.
Reduced
Net Return
: Interest charges, fees, and loan-related expenses reduce your
net return
, even if the investment makes money.
Greater Sensitivity to Market Shifts
: Leverage increases volatility. Even small changes in asset value can have an outsized effect on performance, which demands careful attention to your
risk tolerance
.
Using Leverage Strategically
Model Worst-Case Scenarios
Before leveraging an investment, estimate the potential consequences if the asset declines in value. Include interest costs, transaction fees, and possible exit penalties in your calculation.
Match Leverage to Your Risk Profile
Leverage is not suitable for every investor. Make sure it fits your goals and timeline.
Use Planning Tools to Make Informed Decisions

Leverage is a tool. Applying carefully can boost your
expected ROI
and help you locate a
better investment
. But if used recklessly, it can wipe out capital with impunity.

Alternatives to the ROI Formula
There are many alternatives to the very generic return on investment ratio.
The most detailed measure of return is known as the
Internal Rate of Return (IRR).
This is a measure of all the cash flow received over the life of an investment, expressed as an annual percentage (%) growth rate. This metric takes into account the timing of cash flows, which is a preferred measure of return in sophisticated industries like
private equity and venture capital
.
Other alternatives to ROI include
Return on Equity (ROE)
and
Return on Assets (ROA)
. These two ratios don’t consider the timing of cash flows and represent only an annual rate of return (as opposed to a lifetime rate of return like IRR). However, they are more specific than the generic return on investment since the denominator is more clearly specified. Equity and Assets have a specific meaning, while “investment” can mean different things.
ROI Is a Starting Point, Not the Whole Analysis
A single ROI percentage can make a decision look more obvious than it is. Before acting on it, check what the number leaves out: the holding period, how the investment was financed, and the costs covered above that are easy to miss on a first pass.
Investors and analysts who move beyond a one-number comparison typically build out a fuller model: cash flows, financing assumptions, and multiple scenarios rather than a single ratio. That broader skill set is what CFI’s
FMVA® Certification
is built to teach, for anyone who wants their investment analysis to hold up past the first calculation.

Frequently Asked Questions
A strong understanding of ROI helps you make confident, informed financial choices. Below are answers to common questions that will support your ability to apply the
return on investment formula
.
What is a realistic ROI?
Realistic ROI expectations vary by asset type, holding period, and your personal risk profile. For example, long-term portfolios of index funds may return 7% to 10% annually, while higher-risk categories like real estate or startups may promise more, but with greater volatility. Context and consistency are more important than chasing high numbers.
Is there an Excel formula for ROI?
Yes. In Excel, you can
calculate ROI
by dividing your
net return
by the original investment amount. For instance, if you invest $1,000 and your final value is $1,250, your gain is $250. ROI equals 250 divided by 1,000, which gives you 25%.
Here is the basic Excel formula for ROI:
ROI =(Final_Value – Initial_Investment) / Initial_Investment X 100%
What is the ROI percentage?
ROI percentage shows how much you earned compared to how much you invested, expressed as a percentage. If you invest $1,000 and sell for $1,200, the gain is $200. Dividing 200 by 1,000 gives you an ROI of 20%.
What is ROI in Business?
ROI in business works the same as calculating ROI on any type of investment. Measuring ROI is critical for setting goals, measuring performance, and making organizational decisions.
What is a simple ROI calculation?
You calculate ROI by subtracting the original cost from the final value, then dividing that result by the original cost. Let’s say you invest $500 and it grows to $600. That’s a $100 gain; 100 divided by 500 gives you a 20% ROI.
What is the best way to calculate ROI?
The right method depends on what you’re measuring. For quick snapshots, use the basic ROI calculation. An annualized ROI offers a more accurate reflection of performance over time for comparing investments with different timelines. In detailed scenarios, like property, product launches, or portfolio analysis, factor in every cost to determine the most accurate return.
