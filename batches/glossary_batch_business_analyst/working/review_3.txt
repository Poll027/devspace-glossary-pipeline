FACT f1 Surveys are best suited to collecting quantitative attitudinal data rather than detailed accounts of behavior.
FACT f2 Include questions needed to answer the research aims rather than collecting information simply because it is easy to ask for.
FACT f3 Write questions that respondents can answer accurately and avoid language that leads them toward a preferred answer.
tb1 Suppose an order-service team wants to understand preferences across several branches. The research aim is to compare how important respondents consider finding delivery instructions quickly. A survey fits that attitudinal question better than a request for a detailed account of everything someone actually does during dispatch.
tb2 Surveys are best suited to collecting quantitative attitudinal data rather than detailed accounts of behavior.
tb3 Include questions needed to answer the research aims rather than collecting information simply because it is easy to ask for. Write questions that respondents can answer accurately and avoid language that leads them toward a preferred answer.
tb4 An example question could be “How important is access to the latest delivery instructions when preparing dispatch?” with clearly stated response options. Another could ask how satisfied the respondent is with current access. Keep questions tied to the stated research aim. Asking about unrelated office preferences would collect information without answering the requirements question.

Compare “How satisfied are you with the current instruction lookup?” with “How much do you love our improved lookup?” The second pushes toward a favorable answer. Also check whether respondents can accurately answer what is being asked: a person who does not prepare dispatch may not be able to describe that experience. In this exercise, the survey’s output is quantified attitudes and preferences about instruction access. It is not a reconstruction of the actual sequence of dispatch work. Maintain that distinction when explaining the results to stakeholders so the requirements discussion does not treat a preference score as a detailed behavioral account.
tb5 A large set of responses does not change the kind of evidence collected. Stated attitudes remain attitudes; they are not detailed observations of behavior. Easy-to-ask questions also do not belong automatically: each should serve the research aims.
