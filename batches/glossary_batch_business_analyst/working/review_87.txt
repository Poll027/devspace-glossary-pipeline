FACT f1 Five Whys traces a problem’s cause by repeatedly asking why the previous explanation occurred.
FACT f2 Write the problem, ask why it happened, record the answer, and ask why that answer happened to extend the causal chain.
FACT f3 Five is a guide rather than a required stopping point; use the chain to reach an underlying cause rather than restating the symptom.
tb1 Imagine the problem statement is “Dispatch preparation is delayed by clarification calls.” Ask why those calls occurred. In this hypothetical causal chain, staff could not find current instructions. Ask why that happened: amended instructions remained in a separate record. Ask why they remained separate: the handoff did not identify where the current instruction record should be updated.
tb2 Five Whys traces a problem’s cause by repeatedly asking why the previous explanation occurred.
tb3 Write the problem, ask why it happened, record the answer, and ask why that answer happened to extend the causal chain. Five is a guide rather than a required stopping point; use the chain to reach an underlying cause rather than restating the symptom.
tb4 Write each answer and use it as the starting point for the next why question. The chain moves from the stated problem toward an underlying cause instead of repeatedly renaming “dispatch delay.” These answers are assumptions provided for the exercise, not a factual diagnosis of a real team’s process.

Five is a guide, not a required stopping point. Do not invent a fifth explanation merely to reach the technique’s name, and do not stop automatically at five if the chain has not reached an underlying cause. Keep the successive explanation visible so a reader can follow how the cause of the previous explanation is being examined.

The useful output is a recorded causal chain tied to the original problem. The technique is not a guarantee that the first plausible answer is the true cause. Repeated why questions should extend the underlying explanation rather than restate the symptom.
tb5 “Why?” followed by the same symptom in different words does not extend the causal chain. Five is not a compulsory count. Avoid manufacturing answers to fill five lines or treating the technique’s name as proof that the fifth answer must be the root cause.
