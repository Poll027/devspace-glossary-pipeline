tb1 Imagine a business model containing Customer and Order. Draw a named box for each entity and list relevant attributes. For this hypothetical diagram, Customer contains CustomerID and Name; Order contains OrderID, CustomerID, and RequestedDate. Clearly identify CustomerID as Customer’s primary key, OrderID as Order’s primary key, and Order.CustomerID as a foreign key.
tb2 A model diagram can show entity types, their attributes, and relationships between instances.
tb3 Draw named entity boxes with relevant attributes, clearly identify primary-key and foreign-key attributes, and connect related entities through each foreign key and its referenced key, marking the permitted multiplicities. Use multiplicity labels such as 1, 0..1, 0..*, or 1..* to state the permitted numbers of related instances.
tb4 Connect the foreign-key attribute to its referenced key. Mark permitted multiplicities on the relationship: suppose every order belongs to exactly one customer and each customer may have zero or many orders. Use 1 at the Customer end and 0..* at the Order end for those rules. A 0..1 label would instead allow zero or one; 1..* would require one or many.

Read the diagram back in business language. It says which things are described, what properties they have, how their instances relate, and how many related instances are permitted. The links should correspond to the identified keys rather than being arbitrary lines between boxes.

The result is an entity-relationship diagram, not just a list of tables. The particular attributes and mandatory customer rule are invented for this exercise. In actual modeling, use the established domain information and permitted counts rather than borrowing this example’s rules automatically.
tb5 Two boxes joined by an unlabeled line do not communicate the full permitted multiplicities. Likewise, identifying keys without connecting a foreign key to its referenced key leaves the intended relationship unclear. Keep both structural links and allowed counts visible.
