FACT f1 A decision tree represents choices and possible outcomes with branches.

tb1 Suppose a hypothetical dispatch decision first asks whether an order is urgent. If yes, it next asks whether approval is present. If no, it proceeds through the nonurgent outcome. A decision tree makes that sequence of choices and possible outcomes visible with branches.
tb2 A decision tree represents choices and possible outcomes with branches.
tb3 Start with the initial decision, add branches for the possible answers, and continue with subsequent decisions and their outcomes.
tb4 Start with the initial decision “Urgent?” Add one branch for yes and another for no. On the yes branch, add the subsequent decision “Approval present?” and branches for its possible answers. The approved path ends at “Proceed”; the unapproved path ends at “Request approval.” In this exercise, the no-urgency branch ends at “Proceed.” These are invented policy outcomes, not general rules for dispatch.

Read a path from the initial decision through the relevant answers to its outcome. An urgent order without approval follows yes at the first decision and no at the second, reaching Request approval. This explanation ties branch labels to decisions instead of using unlabeled lines.

The useful output is sequential branching logic. Do not add a subsequent decision to every branch merely to make the drawing symmetrical; continue where the decision being represented requires it. The tree communicates choices and outcomes, not the statistical prediction methods that may use a similarly named structure elsewhere.
tb5 A branch represents a possible answer or choice leading onward; it is not just decoration around a list of outcomes. Keep the initial and subsequent decisions visible so the reader can follow the sequence. This is business decision logic, not a machine-learning lesson.
