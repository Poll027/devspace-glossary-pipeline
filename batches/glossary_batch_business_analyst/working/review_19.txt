tb1 Suppose the operations manager has high power over an instruction-access change and high interest in its outcome. Dispatch staff have high interest but lower power. A finance approver has high power but limited day-to-day interest, while an indirectly affected group has low power and low interest. These are example assessments, not assumptions about everyone with those titles.
tb2 Plot stakeholders on a grid with separate low-to-high power and interest axes.
tb3 Manage high-power, high-interest stakeholders closely. Keep high-power, low-interest stakeholders satisfied. Keep low-power, high-interest stakeholders informed. Monitor low-power, low-interest stakeholders with less intensive engagement.
tb4 Plot them on a grid with a low-to-high power axis and a separate low-to-high interest axis. The manager’s high/high position calls for close management. The high-power, low-interest approver should be kept satisfied. Dispatch staff in the low-power, high-interest position should be kept informed. The low/low group is monitored with less intensive engagement.

Read each position using both axes. Low power does not imply low interest, and high power does not automatically imply high interest. In the example, dispatch staff and the finance approver occupy different quadrants even though both matter to the initiative.

The matrix turns the assessed combination into an engagement approach. It does not identify stakeholders on its own or prescribe a universal message frequency. If you describe the dispatch group as needing information, point back to its assessed low-power/high-interest position rather than simply claiming all users belong there. The practical skill is matching the action to the plotted combination while keeping the assessments explicit.
tb5 Do not collapse power and interest into one importance label. “Keep informed” and “keep satisfied” belong to different combinations. A job title alone is not a substitute for assessing and plotting the stakeholder’s power and interest in this work.
