tb1 Suppose a stakeholder asks, “Which customer placed each order, and which requested delivery dates belong to those orders?” Translate that information need into the entities and attributes that can supply it.
tb2 Business entities and their descriptive attributes give a model the information used to describe a business domain.
tb3 Identify the reporting or filtering information needed and match each item to the entity attributes that can supply it.
tb4 In this hypothetical domain, Customer.Name supplies the customer description, Order.RequestedDate supplies the requested date, and the established customer-order relationship connects the two. If the stakeholder wants to filter by branch, look for the entity attribute that actually records branch. If none has been identified, do not pretend the information is available just because the report needs it.

Record a mapping such as “customer name → Customer.Name” and “requested delivery date → Order.RequestedDate.” This makes the relationship between the reporting or filtering request and modeled information explicit. The mapping is conceptual work here; it does not require writing SQL or opening a dashboard tool.

The practical output is a supported account of which attributes provide the requested business information. A stakeholder phrase and an attribute name need not be identical, so explain the match in plain language. Conversely, matching words alone is not enough if the attribute describes something else. Keep the business question visible while checking the model’s entities and descriptive attributes.
tb5 Wanting a report does not establish that all needed attributes exist. Match each reporting or filtering need to attributes that can supply it; do not silently invent missing information or introduce query syntax before it is taught.
