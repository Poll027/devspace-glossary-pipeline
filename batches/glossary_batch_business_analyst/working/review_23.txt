tb1 Suppose sales wants very few fields on a delivery-instruction form, while dispatch wants enough information to avoid clarification calls. Their goals and measures of success differ: one emphasizes easy entry, the other complete dispatch information. Naming that difference is more useful than presenting one side’s preferred design as an isolated solution.
tb2 Different stakeholders may have conflicting goals and different measures of success.
tb3 Build shared understanding by involving stakeholders in discussion of goals and evidence rather than presenting an isolated solution. Forced ranking or dot voting can help a group move toward consensus.
tb4 Bring stakeholders into discussion of the goals and evidence. For this hypothetical case, examine examples of instruction entry and clarification work together. Ask what each group is trying to achieve and which evidence relates to that goal. The purpose is shared understanding of the competing needs, not hiding the disagreement behind a vague claim that everyone wants a better process.

Forced ranking or dot voting can help the group move toward consensus. For example, participants could rank the information needs being discussed or place dots beside the needs they consider most important. The technique supports movement toward agreement; a ranking does not itself establish that the selected solution is correct.

The useful BA contribution is a discussion in which stakeholders can see one another’s goals and evidence before moving toward a shared position. Keep any remaining disagreement visible rather than announcing consensus merely because a voting activity took place. The example fields and goals are hypothetical prompts for that discussion.
tb5 Consensus building is not presenting a ready-made solution and asking for compliance. Voting or ranking can help, but the supported claim is that these techniques can move a group toward consensus—not that a vote guarantees complete agreement or correctness.
