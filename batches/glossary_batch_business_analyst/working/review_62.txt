FACT f1 A business case justifies an initiative by describing the problem, possible solutions, expected benefits, and costs.
FACT f2 List and estimate relevant costs and benefits over the period being evaluated, including direct and indirect effects.
FACT f4 Present the recommendation with the supporting financial assessment in the business case.
FACT f6 For a monetary-return estimate, calculate projected net return = total projected monetary benefits over the evaluation period − project cost, including the cost of the project itself rather than reporting gross savings alone.
FACT f7 Interpret a positive projected monetary return as estimated benefits exceeding cost over that period, and report the assumptions and time horizon because the return is an estimate, not a promised financial outcome.
FACT f8 Calculate percentage ROI = (monetary benefits − costs) / costs × 100%, using net monetary return as the numerator and investment cost as the denominator.
FACT f9 Use the costs and monetary benefits for the stated evaluation period, include relevant costs rather than just a headline purchase cost, and use the same time period when comparing alternatives.
FACT f10 Net monetary return is the benefits-minus-costs amount in money, whereas percentage ROI expresses that net return relative to investment cost.
tb1 Imagine a business case for improving delivery-instruction access. Describe the dispatch problem, possible solutions, and expected benefits and costs. For this hypothetical one-year assessment, estimated monetary benefits are 18,000 and relevant project costs total 12,000, including implementation and other relevant costs rather than only a headline purchase price.
tb2 A business case justifies an initiative by describing the problem, possible solutions, expected benefits, and costs.
tb3 List and estimate relevant costs and benefits over the period being evaluated, including direct and indirect effects. Present the recommendation with the supporting financial assessment in the business case. For a monetary-return estimate, calculate projected net return = total projected monetary benefits over the evaluation period − project cost, including the cost of the project itself rather than reporting gross savings alone. Interpret a positive projected monetary return as estimated benefits exceeding cost over that period, and report the assumptions and time horizon because the return is an estimate, not a promised financial outcome. Calculate percentage ROI = (monetary benefits − costs) / costs × 100%, using net monetary return as the numerator and investment cost as the denominator. Use the costs and monetary benefits for the stated evaluation period, include relevant costs rather than just a headline purchase cost, and use the same time period when comparing alternatives. Net monetary return is the benefits-minus-costs amount in money, whereas percentage ROI expresses that net return relative to investment cost.
tb4 Projected net monetary return is benefits minus costs: 18,000 − 12,000 = 6,000. Percentage ROI is that net return divided by investment cost, times 100%: 6,000 / 12,000 × 100% = 50%. The 6,000 amount and 50% percentage describe different things. Gross benefits of 18,000 are not the net return.

A positive projected monetary return means the estimated benefits exceed cost over the stated period. Report the one-year horizon and the assumptions behind the estimates; this is not a promised financial outcome. If another alternative is compared, use costs and monetary benefits for the same evaluation period, including relevant direct and indirect effects.

Present the recommendation with its supporting financial assessment in the business case. Do not let the calculation replace the problem and option descriptions. The useful output explains why the initiative is being considered and what estimated return supports the recommendation, with the costs, period, and assumptions visible to readers.
tb5 ROI’s numerator is net monetary return, not gross savings. Its denominator is investment cost, not benefits. A positive projection is an estimate under stated assumptions and a time horizon, not a promise. Net return in money and percentage ROI must not be labeled interchangeably.
