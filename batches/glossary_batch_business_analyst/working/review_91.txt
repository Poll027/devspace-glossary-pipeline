tb1 Imagine a Power BI model with CustomerDim and OrderFact. A visual uses customer descriptions and order amounts, so the model relationship needs to propagate filters between related tables.
tb2 Model relationships propagate filters between related tables so a visual can use fields from multiple tables.
tb3 Use Modeling > Manage relationships > New and choose the two tables and their matching columns. Set cardinality according to the uniqueness of values on each side and check the active relationship setting. Choose cross-filter direction deliberately: Single propagates filters in one direction and Both allows propagation in both directions.
tb4 Use Modeling > Manage relationships > New and choose the two tables and matching columns. For this hypothetical data, CustomerDim.CustomerID is unique and OrderFact.CustomerID repeats across orders, so set cardinality according to that uniqueness and check the active relationship setting.

Choose cross-filter direction deliberately. Single propagates filters in one direction; Both allows propagation in both directions. These are different choices, not an instruction to select Both whenever a report has more than one table. The direction must be considered for the intended filter behavior.

The useful output is a relationship whose matching columns, cardinality, active setting, and filtering direction are understood. If a report combines fields from both tables, explain how the relationship enables filters to reach related data rather than assuming that similar column names are enough. The example stops at the supported relationship settings and does not invent automatic conflict resolution or a universal recommendation for every model.
tb5 A relationship is not just a line for display: it propagates filters. Also, Both is not automatically the best setting; it permits two-way propagation and should be chosen deliberately. Check uniqueness and the active setting rather than assuming them.
