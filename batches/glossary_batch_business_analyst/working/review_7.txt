tb1 Suppose stakeholders struggle to explain what they need on a delivery-instruction screen. Instead of implementing the screen, draw a simple representation for discussion. A low-fidelity prototype can be inexpensive and relatively simple while still letting stakeholders explore a design.
tb2 A low-fidelity prototype is an inexpensive, relatively simple representation used to explore and test a design.
tb3 Sketch a wireframe by drawing the screen’s main content areas and controls without detailed visual styling. Use a prototype to gather feedback about a design before investing in a fully implemented version.
tb4 For this example, sketch a page with an order identifier at the top, a large area for current instructions, and controls labeled “Select order” and “Confirm instructions.” These labels and areas are invented design choices for the exercise. Leave out detailed colors, typography, and visual styling. The wireframe’s job here is to represent the main content areas and controls, not to establish the final appearance.

Use the sketch to gather feedback before investing in a fully implemented version. A dispatcher might point to the instruction area and ask for the last amendment to be visible; another might explain that the confirmation control does not match their intended task. Record what the feedback reveals rather than treating every drawn control as an agreed requirement. The representation gives stakeholders something concrete to respond to while the design is still being explored. After discussion, the useful output is feedback about the design and needs it surfaces, not a claim that the screen is complete simply because all its boxes have labels.
tb5 A wireframe need not look like a finished interface. Adding detailed styling is not what makes this low-fidelity elicitation useful. Also distinguish the proposed design from the feedback collected about it: a sketched control is not automatically an agreed requirement.
