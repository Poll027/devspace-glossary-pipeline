FACT f1 Observation examines what users do in their work context rather than relying solely on their account of what they do.
FACT f2 Watch the user perform work, then ask questions about what was observed.
FACT f3 Record the tasks performed and analyze their sequence and subtasks to understand how the goal is achieved.
FACT f4 Record the user’s stated process, compare it with the steps observed during actual work, and log discrepancies with notes of the observed actions that support them.
tb1 Suppose a dispatcher says, “I open the order and send it to the driver.” Observe the dispatcher doing the work in its actual context, then ask about the actions you saw. In this example, you observe the dispatcher open the order, consult a separate instruction sheet, call sales to clarify an address, and then send the order to the driver.
tb2 Observation examines what users do in their work context rather than relying solely on their account of what they do.
tb3 Observe the user perform work, then ask questions about what was observed. Record the tasks performed and analyze their sequence and subtasks to understand how the goal is achieved. Record the user’s stated process, compare it with the steps observed during actual work, and log discrepancies with notes of the observed actions that support them.
tb4 Record the performed tasks and their sequence. You can describe the goal as preparing an order for dispatch and identify subtasks such as finding instructions and resolving uncertainty. Ask questions about those observed steps: “What information were you checking on the separate sheet?” or “What made the clarification call necessary?” These questions follow the observation instead of replacing it with a guessed explanation.

Keep the stated process alongside the observed process. The statement omitted the separate-sheet lookup and clarification call; log that discrepancy with notes of the observed actions that support it. Do not change the observation record to make the two accounts agree. The comparison helps you understand how this work was actually performed in the observed situation. It does not establish that every dispatcher always follows the same sequence. The useful result is a supported account of tasks and discrepancies that can inform requirements, not a judgment about whether the participant gave a good interview answer.
tb5 Observation is more than asking someone what they normally do. It examines work as performed and then uses questions to understand what was seen. A difference between an account and observed steps should be documented with action notes, not replaced by speculation about motive.
