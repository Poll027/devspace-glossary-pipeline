FACT f1 Process mapping can expose bottlenecks, redundant efforts, and unnecessary steps.
FACT f2 Inspect the mapped steps and handoffs for obstacles that impede efficient flow.
FACT f3 Compare current-state and future-state maps to identify specific differences and potential efficiency gains.
FACT f4 List the specific steps or handoffs where the map shows delay, redundancy, or missing work, and review these findings with process stakeholders.
FACT f5 Identify a bottleneck as a capacity-limited step that cannot keep pace, causing work to accumulate and limiting the whole workflow’s throughput, and cite the observed capacity, throughput, or accumulating-work evidence in the diagram annotation.
tb1 Suppose the dispatch map shows orders accumulating before an approval step. A delay label alone does not establish the full bottleneck explanation. In this hypothetical case, the step can handle ten orders per hour while fifteen arrive, and the waiting work grows. Annotate the map with that capacity and accumulating-work evidence: the step cannot keep pace and limits the workflow’s throughput.
tb2 Process mapping can expose bottlenecks, redundant efforts, and unnecessary steps.
tb3 Inspect the mapped steps and handoffs for obstacles that impede efficient flow. Compare current-state and future-state maps to identify specific differences and potential efficiency gains. List the specific steps or handoffs where the map shows delay, redundancy, or missing work, and review these findings with process stakeholders. Identify a bottleneck as a capacity-limited step that cannot keep pace, causing work to accumulate and limiting the whole workflow’s throughput, and cite the observed capacity, throughput, or accumulating-work evidence in the diagram annotation.
tb4 Inspect other mapped steps and handoffs for obstacles, redundancy, or missing work. Perhaps the same instructions are copied at two handoffs. List the specific steps where the map shows those issues and review the findings with process stakeholders. Do not group every issue under “bottleneck” merely because it is inconvenient.

Compare current-state and future-state maps too. If the proposal removes one copying step, identify that concrete difference and discuss its potential efficiency gain. A map comparison identifies a proposed difference; it is not itself a measured result.

The output should connect each finding to a location in the process and supporting evidence. For the approval bottleneck, the key evidence is capacity that cannot keep pace, accumulated work, and the resulting throughput limitation. For redundant copying, the finding is repeated effort. These distinctions make the analysis more informative than marking every slow-looking box red.
tb5 Not every delay is a bottleneck. The supported bottleneck definition requires a capacity-limited step that cannot keep pace, causing accumulation and limiting throughput. Also label future-state efficiency gains as potential until supported by actual results.
