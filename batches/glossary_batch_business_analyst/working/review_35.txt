FACT f1 In a one-to-many (1:M) relationship, one row on the 'one' side relates to many rows on the 'many' side; for example, one department has many employees.
FACT f2 In the Customer/Order example, a customer can place a minimum of zero and a maximum of many orders, while an order relates to a maximum of one customer, so Order is the 'many' side (the many side is optional, since a customer doesn't need to place an order to be a customer); the foreign key CustID is placed in the Order table and must match a valid CustID in the Customer table.
tb1 Suppose Customer has one row for customer 101 and one for customer 102. Order has three rows referencing 101 and no rows referencing 102. One customer can therefore relate to many orders, while the example allows a customer to have zero orders.
tb2 In a one-to-many (1:M) relationship, one row on the 'one' side relates to many rows on the 'many' side; for example, one department has many employees.
tb3 In the Customer/Order example, a customer can place a minimum of zero and a maximum of many orders, while an order relates to a maximum of one customer, so Order is the 'many' side (the many side is optional, since a customer doesn't need to place an order to be a customer); the foreign key CustID is placed in the Order table and must match a valid CustID in the Customer table.
tb4 Identify Order as the many side. Its CustID foreign key stores the reference to a valid Customer CustID. The same customer identifier can appear on multiple order rows because those rows are different orders belonging to the same customer. The Customer side has the customer row being referenced.

Read the allowed counts in both directions. Customer 101 has several orders; customer 102 has none and still exists as a customer. Each order relates to a maximum of one customer in the stated example. Do not reverse the relationship merely because the customer name is shown on an order report.

The useful modeling decision is not just naming “one-to-many.” It is identifying which thing can have many related instances and placing the reference on that many side. The zero-order customer also shows why “many” should not be interpreted as requiring at least one order in this example. These counts are the business rules of the Customer/Order example, not universal counts for every relationship.
tb5 One-to-many does not mean every one-side row already has several related rows. The stated customer example permits zero orders. Nor should the foreign key be placed on Customer just because that is the one side: CustID belongs in Order for this relationship.
