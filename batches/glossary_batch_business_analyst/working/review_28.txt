FACT f1 Lean process improvement aims to eliminate waste and improve value delivered by a process.

tb1 Imagine the process map shows dispatch instructions copied twice before preparation. The team investigates that repeated effort as a possible non-value-adding step. Lean improvement aims to eliminate waste and improve the value delivered by the process; the example is about examining a specific source of waste, not removing work simply because it takes time.
tb2 Lean process improvement aims to eliminate waste and improve value delivered by a process.
tb3 Inspect a process for non-value-adding steps and propose a change that removes or reduces the identified waste. Agree an improvement action with the team, implement it, and review its effects to continue improving the process.
tb4 Inspect the copying steps and propose a change that removes or reduces the identified waste. In this hypothetical situation, the team proposes using the current instruction record instead of making a second copy. Agree the improvement action with the team, implement it, and review its effects.

The review might examine whether the repeated copying was reduced and whether the process now delivers the intended value more effectively. Those are questions for the review, not results you can infer from the proposal alone. If the effects show further issues, continue improving the process rather than treating the first change as the final answer.

The BA’s analysis connects an observed process feature, identified waste, an agreed action, and a review of effects. The technique remains grounded in the work: “remove a box from the map” is less informative than explaining which non-value-adding activity is being reduced and then checking the change.
tb5 Improvement is not synonymous with deleting as many steps as possible. Inspect for identified non-value-adding work, agree an action, and review its effects after implementation. A proposed simplification is not yet evidence that value or efficiency improved.
